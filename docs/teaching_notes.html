Live Titanic demonstration

Use the scaffold. The completed notebook is the reference copy. Both versions have matching headings. The main path is intentionally short: survivor counts, survival proportions, then a grouped comparison using color.

  • Preview a few rows and confirm the variables used today are complete.
  • Skip the notebook’s optional fare histogram because it duplicates the warmup.
  • Count survivors by class. Ask what information the plot leaves unresolved.
  • Return to all passengers and calculate survival proportions. Explain the denominator and why the mean of a complete 0/1 field is a proportion.
  • Add sex to the grouping, with color distinguishing the source categories. Ask students for a prediction before running it.
  • End by proposing age as a next question. Avoid turning the demonstration into a survey of every Titanic variable.

A slightly deeper tornado orientation

The three-minute introduction covers details that influence the analysis:

  1. One row is one whole tornado track. Extra state/county continuation records and one exact duplicate were removed in preparation. A track can cross state lines. Starting state does not list all states affected.
  2. Types carry meaning. The identifier is a label, EF is ordered, state is nominal, and length and width are quantities with different units. Do not infer measurement meaning from a column’s storage dtype alone.
  3. Clock time is CST for every record. Comparing CST hours does not automatically compare local daylight or nighttime.
  4. Missing ratings stay missing. EF0 is valid. There are 111 unknown ratings, leaving 4,595 records with an observed rating. Students should decide whether their question requires those values before filtering.

EF categories reflect damage surveys and estimated winds. They are not direct wind measurements, and a larger category number is not a ratio of wind speeds. Source: https://www.weather.gov/oun/efscale.

The starter shows a few rows, column information and missingness, but no substantive tornado analysis. This gives students a foundation without steering their question choice.

The investigation

During brainstorming, reward distinct questions that students can connect to available fields. Offer general prompts only if needed: distribution, comparison, relationship, time or space, unusual observations, and missingness.

When groups select a question, ask which rows, variables, units, comparison, and any denominator would provide evidence. Words like dangerous or worst need a measurable definition.

At around minute 25 ask what the first visualization leaves unresolved. At around minute 32 ask everyone to prepare their plot and explanation for regrouping.

Circulation prompts:

  • What do you actually see in this view?
  • What does that leave unresolved?
  • How many records support the comparison?
  • Which observations drive the result?
  • What other view would help distinguish your explanations?

Use the separate plotting reference for syntax support. Avoid supplying a shared tornado question or a worked tornado result.

Sharing and closing

Randomly regroup students, aiming for groups of 3-4 from different original teams. Allow one minute to move, about two minutes per person, and remaining time for follow-up questions. Every student explains their original team’s work independently.

The closing asks for a visualization that changed a question, something the data could not fully answer, or a useful approach learned from someone else. A well-explained inconclusive result is useful.
